One in a HundredHow it works · v0.3.1 · 2026-10-15

Percentile facts, graded

Earning $30,000 does not put you in the global top 1%

Under the most generous definition on the site it puts you at the 94th percentile; under the other two, the 72nd and 75th. One in a Hundred exists to settle claims like that: it holds 950 percentile facts across 20 countries and the world, from 72 sources, each with a source link, an access date and a grade for how far independent sources agree. This page explains how those facts are made and what they can't show.

The claim that started it

Three definitions of income, and $30k is the top 1% under none of them

"Earning $30,000 a year puts you in the top 1% of earners worldwide." Sometimes the number is $60,000. The gap between the two versions is the definition of income, so the site keeps each definition as its own measure and never averages them.

History

The top-1% line moved too, and the median moved faster

Under WID's pre-tax definition the world top 1% started at $131,000 in 1980 and $223,000 in 2023, in constant purchasing-power dollars: a real rise of 70%. Over the same years the median adult went from $5,200 to $11,100. Where a source has history, the site draws a second chart like this one under the curve. Open the income fact to see it there.

Two more

Age and the marathon

One in a hundred people alive is at least 84.6 years old; the median person is 31.9. To be in the fastest 1% of the 778,747 people who finished a timed marathon in the 2013 research sample, you needed to run under 2:50. The median finisher took 4:19.

How the numbers are made

One fetcher per source, one row shape, fixed grading rules

Each fact is one distribution: a measure, a group, a reference year, a grid of percentile points, and its sources. One fetcher per source reads it into the same row shape for every year it publishes. A build step makes the latest year the fact and earlier years its history, from the same sources only. A verify step computes the spread across sources at each shown percentile, assigns the grade by fixed rules, and refuses to build a fact with no source.

The UN pyramid gives ages directly. WID publishes income and wealth thresholds from 1980. The World Bank API answers "what welfare level is the 99th percentile" per country. NHANES gives measured US body data, weighted to the population. NCD-RisC gives height means by birth cohort (so those percentiles are derived) and BMI in bands (so those are interpolated). Marathon times are plain quantiles of the 778,747 finishers in the 2013 sample published with Allen, Dechow, Pope and Wu's Management Science paper, compiled from races' own results.

Grades

A
Two or more independent sources, at least one official, within 10% at every shown percentile.
B
One official source, or a derived percentile.
C
Sources disagree by more than 10%, or a known definitional mismatch.
D
From the research loop, not yet checked by a person.

A reviewer can lower a grade with a reason and never raise one. The way up is another source.

Questions outside the catalog

A research loop that ships its answers stamped "unreviewed"

A question the catalog can't answer goes to a research loop: a fixed prompt, a JSON schema, and a model with web search that must name its sources, define the measure, and say where the sources disagree. The result ships as a grade D fact with an "unreviewed" stamp on the page and on its share image, and a person reviews it later.

Sixty popular questions (chess ratings, IQ, push-ups, typing speed, Instagram followers, VO2 max, savings by age, test scores) have been through it. 42 produced a fact; 41 ship, and the bench press is held because its grid ran backwards. 18 found nothing the model would stand behind. The first result is why the stamp exists: for "what chess rating is the top 1%" the model found four sources and wrote a careful caveat about FIDE's rating floor, then averaged FIDE, US Chess and Lichess values into one grid. Review is where one definition gets chosen.

Live research at the moment of asking is written and waits for a server. Until then a missing fact offers "Request this fact", which files a GitHub issue the loop picks up.

The parser

90.5% on a frozen set of 200 questions, and an honest asterisk

Two parsers return the same shape. A rules parser runs in your browser: regexes for percentiles, sex, age and values, a country alias table, and a phrasing match that accepts inflections, with a fuzzy fallback for typos. An LLM parser, once deployed, runs in a Cloudflare Worker against the same catalog, and the browser falls back to the rules parser after 2.5 seconds or on any error.

Limits

What it cannot show

  • Almost everything is B. An A needs independent producers measuring the same definition, and for income, wealth, height and age there is usually one.
  • Height outside the US is derived from cohort means under a normal assumption. Measured height tails are slightly heavier than normal, so the Dutch 202 cm is probably a centimeter or two high.
  • BMI percentiles are linear interpolations inside published bands.
  • Marathon facts describe the 2013 research sample (61% US); the "world" running group means finishers in that sample.
  • Researched facts are only as good as what the web says. They are graded D and stamped, can mix definitions until reviewed, and have no group size.
  • History charts follow one definition, so a change of method inside the source shows up as an unexplained kink in the line.
  • Group counts for age bands and categories are survey shares applied to UN totals.
  • The LLM parser, live research and the request queue need a Cloudflare account and a Groq key before they run.
