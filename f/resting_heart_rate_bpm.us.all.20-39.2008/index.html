Definition

Resting radial pulse counted by a physician for 30 seconds and doubled, taken after about 4 minutes of seated rest, in the NHANES mobile examination centre. Covers a normative sample that excludes people with a current medical condition or medication that affects pulse rate (beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, thyroid disorders, pregnancy, irregular pulse, likely infection). Weighted to the US civilian noninstitutionalized population. Both sexes, age 20-39.

Only one source was found that publishes a 1st percentile for directly measured resting heart rate in a nationally representative adult sample: NHANES 1999-2008 via NCHS. I found no second independent source on the same definition. The question asks about 'adults'. The NCHS tables give age bands, not an all-adult row. The 1st percentile is stable across adult bands (48 bpm at 20-39, 47 at 40-59 and 60-79), so about 47-48 bpm is the top-1% (lowest) value for US adults of both sexes. Because lower is better here, 'top 1%' maps to the published 1st percentile. Mismatches: (1) the normative sample excludes people on heart-rate-affecting drugs or with relevant conditions, about 28% of those examined, so the full adult population's lower tail could differ slightly, since beta-blocker users would push it lower; (2) it is a single seated clinic reading, not a morning or sleeping value; (3) the data are from 1999-2008. Comparison on a different definition, not averaged in: Quer et al. 2020, PLOS ONE (Scripps, 92,457 US adults with Fitbit wrist trackers, 2016-2018, about 33 million daily resting-HR values) reports that individual average daily resting heart rate ranged from 40 to 109 bpm, with a mean of 65 bpm. Wearable resting HR is computed largely from inactive and sleep periods and runs lower than a seated clinic pulse, so its tails are not comparable. Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7001906/. The SCAPIS study (Heart, 2024; 30,154 Swedish adults aged 50-65) gives 2.5th-97.5th ranges of 57-90 bpm (men) and 61-92 bpm (women), but for 24-hour ambulatory heart rate, which is also a different definition.
