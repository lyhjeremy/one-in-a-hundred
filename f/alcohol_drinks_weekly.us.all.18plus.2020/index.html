Definition

Self-reported usual number of standard drinks per week over the past 12 months, as a cumulative distribution of all US adults including past-year abstainers (0 drinks). Rounded to the nearest percentile.

The question asks about the top 10% of drinkers, but both sources rank all US adults, including the 28% (2020) to 33% (2015) who did not drink in the past year. So the p90 of 14 drinks a week (2020) is the cutoff for the top 10% of all adults. Restricting to drinkers would push the cutoff higher. In the 2020 table, the top 10% of drinkers is roughly the top 7% of all adults, which lands in the 18-19 drinks per week bin. That figure is read from the table, not published by ARG. Both sources are self-reported, and self-reports in US surveys cover only about half of alcohol sales, so true consumption is likely higher. The widely quoted figure that the top 10% of Americans average 74 drinks a week comes from Philip J. Cook's 'Paying the Tab' (2007). It uses NESARC 2001-02 data with each respondent's self-reported drinks multiplied by 1.97 to match sales. It is the mean within the top decile of adults, not a cutoff, so it measures something different and is not listed as a source. Cook's ninth decile averages about 15.3 drinks a week (as reported secondhand by Inc.). For comparison, NIAAA defines heavy drinking as more than 14 drinks a week for men and more than 7 for women. NCHS reported that 5.1% of US adults met the NHIS heavy-drinking definition in 2018.
